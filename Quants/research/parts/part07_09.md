## PART 8 — ATTACKING THE WINNER (skeptical infrastructure-CTO review)

**Q1. Is the problem real?** Yes, with primary evidence: Texas SB6 makes curtailment mandatory for ≥75 MW loads; Google signed 1 GW of DR across five utilities specifically targeting ML workloads; PJM's auction is at the price cap and short of reliability; EPRI runs nine DCFlex demos and a 96 MW flexibility-designed site; NVIDIA productized DSX Flex. Utilities are asking "how much can you shed, how fast, how often, and at what cost to you?" — and operators answer with rules of thumb.

**Q2. Is the data obtainable?** Yes, entirely from public sources: Azure LLM inference traces (2023, 2024), Alibaba PAI and Acme job traces, the NLR 0.1-second H100 power-profile dataset (2026), Zeus/Perseus DVFS curves, ISO prices and DR histories via `gridstatus`, NOAA weather, published tariffs, Electricity Maps carbon. The only proprietary element — a real liquid-cooled rack's thermal parameters — is replaced by published lumped-parameter models with sensitivity analysis. **Verdict: no proprietary data required.**

**Q3. Is it already solved commercially?** *Partially and opaquely.* Emerald AI (Conductor), NVIDIA DSX Flex and Google's internal system exist. But (a) their methods are rule/priority-based or undisclosed; (b) none publishes a cost-priced optimization or a trade-off frontier; (c) none is open, so utilities, regulators (Flex MOSAIC) and academics have no benchmark. The commercial activity is the strongest possible validation that the *problem* is worth money — and the absence of an open, optimal, multi-lever controller is the research gap. The project's stance: "Here is the first open benchmark and an optimal controller; here is how far rules are from optimal."

**Q4. Is the proposed improvement meaningful?** The hypotheses are falsifiable and sized: (i) frontier-based allocation delivers the same MW reduction with materially lower goodput loss than uniform capping (Perseus shows 10–30% of energy is bloat — that is "free" flexibility); (ii) joint control with inference replicas and cooling setpoints extends deliverable flexibility beyond what training DVFS alone gives; (iii) stochastic MPC reduces SLO violations relative to deterministic rules at equal cost. If (i) fails — i.e., uniform capping is nearly as good — that is itself a publishable negative result that industry would value.

**Q5. Can a small team (one student) build it?** Yes, because the pieces are standard: SimPy simulator (~2k lines), Pyomo/HiGHS MILP (~500 lines), LightGBM forecasters (~300 lines), FastAPI/Redis/Grafana stack (containers). MILP instances (≈40 jobs × 4 modes × 24 steps + 10 services) have a few thousand binaries and solve in seconds. Scope guards: no sub-second layer (hardware does it), lumped thermal model, two DR program archetypes, one region at a time.

**Q6. Can results be measured?** Yes — the twin produces every metric in Part 13; policies are compared under identical seeds/traces; frontiers are produced by ε-constraint sweeps; forecast skill is measured by pinball loss and coverage.

**Q7. Does it require proprietary data?** No (Q2).

**Q8. Are results dependent on unrealistic assumptions?** The main risks: (a) DVFS curves from A100/H100 may not match Blackwell; (b) thermal model fidelity; (c) DR program simplifications; (d) job elasticity assumptions. Mitigations: publish results across curve families and thermal parameter ranges; include a "rigid jobs only" scenario; validate the power waveforms against NLR measurements; state all assumptions in a limitations section. Results are reported as *ranges and frontiers*, not point claims.

**Q9. Is it merely a dashboard?** No. The dashboard is a view onto a closed-loop controller with a mathematically defined objective, constraints, and dynamics, evaluated against baselines.

**Q10. Is the optimization mathematically meaningful?** Yes: it is a unit-commitment-like MILP with piecewise-linear concave production curves, linear thermal dynamics, storage dynamics, chance-constrained capacity (via demand quantiles), and switching costs — a well-posed, NP-hard-in-general but practically tractable problem, solved in receding horizon (MPC). RL is used only as a comparison baseline where it is appropriate (learning a policy for a simulator), not forced.

**Q11. Would an infrastructure engineer care?** Yes — the output is an actionable schedule (which jobs, what caps, what setpoints), verifiable MW·h delivered, and a demand-charge impact.

**Q12. Would an AI systems researcher care?** Yes — it extends Perseus/Pollux to cluster-level power allocation, introduces the "flexibility supply curve" abstraction, and ships an open benchmark.

**Q13. Would an AI company care?** Yes — faster energization (months to years), DR revenue, lower demand charges, and a defensible answer to utilities and regulators. Companies named in Part 2 (NVIDIA, Google, Microsoft, Oracle, Emerald, Crusoe, CoreWeave, Digital Realty, EPRI) are investing in exactly this.

**Eliminations:** C4 PACKWATT (insufficient differentiation from Sia/FGD; no power/grid story) and C5 SKYFLEX (SkyServe/SpotServe overlap) are eliminated. C2 COLDPLAN is absorbed as HEADROOM's thermal sub-model. C3 ETTR-MAX remains the fallback and contributes reliability constraints.

## PART 9 — THE SELECTED PROJECT

**HEADROOM — Grid-Interactive, Goodput-Aware Model-Predictive Control of AI Clusters** is selected.

**Research-grade problem statement:**

> Given a GPU cluster running synchronous training jobs with known (profiled) power–throughput frontiers and inference services with latency SLOs and forecastable demand, in a liquid-cooled facility with known thermal dynamics and an optional battery, connected to a grid that (a) charges time-varying energy and demand prices, (b) imposes a site power cap and ramp limits, and (c) issues contracted curtailment events with notice — design a controller that minimizes expected total operating cost (energy, demand charges, flexibility penalties, training delay, SLO violations, reliability/switching) over a rolling horizon, and characterize the Pareto frontier between flexibility delivered and compute value lost. Evaluate the controller against rule-based, fair-capping, batch-shifting and reinforcement-learning baselines in an open, trace-driven digital twin.

**Research questions:**
1. How much power flexibility can a mixed AI cluster deliver at *zero* goodput cost (energy bloat), and how does the marginal cost rise beyond that?
2. Does joint control of training DVFS, inference replicas, cooling setpoints and storage dominate single-lever strategies, and by how much?
3. How much does forecast uncertainty (demand, price) erode the benefit, and does stochastic MPC recover it?
4. What is the reliability price (switches, checkpoints, thermal cycling) of aggressive flexibility, and where should contracts be set?
